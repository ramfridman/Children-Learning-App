# Research report: the Russian course for children aged 3 to 8 (B-44)

*Author: russian-language-teacher, 2026-11-03. Status: draft for the owner. Plan only, no code.*

## 1. The question

**Subject:** Russian, as a language and as literacy in Cyrillic.
**Learners:** any child aged 3 to 8 whose first language and app language is Hebrew (owner's decisions 2 and 3, 2026-11-03): a child new to Russian, a heritage speaker who hears Russian at home and understands some or much of it, and a child who already reads. Placement decides the starting point, not age.
**Bands:** A 3–4 (listening, understanding, speaking), B 5–6 (sounds of speech, syllables, first letters), C 7–8 (syllable reading, words, short texts, writing in print) (`docs/PLAN.md` › Learners & age bands).
**What the app can do:** tap, drag, trace on a phone or tablet, listen to recorded clips, a microphone only on a tap and only when a parent turned voice on; no timers or streaks; the recogniser on the phone today is a Hebrew model (`app/lib/speech/sherpa_engine.dart`, ivrit.ai), so there is no Russian speech recognition yet.
**Decisions the research must support:** (1) the method for teaching reading in Russian; (2) the letter order; (3) how the heritage and new-learner routes differ; (4) how writing is taught on a phone (print or cursive, trace or free writing); (5) the errors to diagnose; (6) placement; (7) what the voice must do.

## 3. What the sources agree on

### 3.1 Reading in Russian: the sound-analytic method
- **Reading is rebuilding the sound form of a word from its letters** (Эльконин, quoted in ZHU). So the child first learns to hear and model the **sounds** of a word, before letters. The Russian kindergarten tradition and the federal kindergarten programme (FOP, second-hand) put phonemic hearing and sound analysis before letters, and grade 1 opens with a pre-letter period (GOR). The international evidence agrees: phonemic-awareness teaching helps reading, most when tied to letters (NRP), and in Russian specifically phonemic awareness predicts orthographic coding (RAK).
- **The Elkonin–Zhurova steps** (ZHU): (1) intonational highlighting: the child says a word stretching each sound in turn (с-с-сом, со-о-ом, сом-м-м); (2) a **sound scheme**: a row of boxes, one per sound, filled with neutral chips; (3) chips by kind: **vowels red, hard consonants blue, soft consonants green**; (4) stress and syllables; (5) only then letters, first the vowels, with a **vowel strip** placed on the scheme to change the word (кот → кит); (6) reading syllables with the movable vowel strip arranged in pairs (а–я, у–ю, о–ё) so the child learns that the vowel letter tells how to say the consonant before it. This is the core of Russian syllable reading: the reader looks ahead to the vowel letter (the "positional principle" of Russian graphics).
- **Age:** Zhurova found 3-year-olds take sound tasks only inside a game; 4-year-olds enjoy changing articulation in play; **5-year-olds** take to sound analysis as a task in itself; at 6 the children change words with all vowel letters and read syllables and words (ZHU). This matches our bands: Band A plays with rhyme, syllables and the first sound; Band B does sound analysis and the first letters; Band C reads.
- **The syllable merge (слияние) is the unit of early reading** in Russian primers: a consonant plus a vowel read as one (ма, мя), not letter by letter. Reading stages run from letter-by-letter to syllable reading to whole words (Т. Г. Егоров, «Психология овладения навыком чтения», known to me and seen only in secondary summaries here; I do not cite its details).

### 3.2 Letters: why a non-alphabetical order
- Russian primers introduce letters **not in alphabet order** but by frequency and ease of pronunciation (GOR; the Жукова primer is described the same way). Goretsky's Азбука starts with five vowels (А О И Ы У) and the frequent consonants Н, С, К, Т. The Жукова order quoted in a search result starts А, У, О, М, С, Х, Р, Ш, Ы, Л, Н, К, Т, И, П… (secondary source; not verified against the book).
- Corpus frequency (NKRY) puts о е а и н т с р в л к м д п у first: a child who knows these 15 letters can read a large share of letters in running text.
- Stretchable consonants (м, с, н, л, р, ш) make the merge easy to hear (ммма); stops (к, т, п) cannot be stretched and come after the child can merge. This is common primer practice (the Жукова order starts with М and С); the evidence for it is practice wisdom, not trials.

### 3.3 Heritage learners
- Heritage children **understand far more than they produce and usually read nothing**: in Israel only a minority of heritage children have literacy in Russian (a 2021 survey cited second-hand: 41 %; not opened). They need a different route from beginners: build on comprehension and sound knowledge, move fast to literacy, and work on the known grammar gaps (KD01, PK07, KDP20).
- The gaps that research names for Israeli and other heritage Russian children are **case (accusative)** and **grammatical gender** (MEIR, ROD); vocabulary shrinks where Hebrew dominates (SCH12). Form-focused, playful attention to these, inside meaningful chunks, is recommended (KDP20).
- **Reading Russian helps reading Hebrew**, not the reverse fear (SLS, SCH08). This is a message for parents in the parent area.
- Programmes: Israel has community-run Russian complementary schools and bilingual kindergartens, not a state programme (MEIR; SCH12); Germany has heritage-language lessons in some states, with mixed groups and foreign-language textbooks (HSU); the US research is mostly about university heritage learners (KD01, PK07). **No source gave a ready-made, researched heritage curriculum for ages 3–8.** I built our heritage route from the principles above and the Russian kindergarten and grade-1 sequence.

### 3.4 Russian as a foreign language for young children
- CEFR Pre-A1 (CEFR 2020) is the yardstick for Band A and early Band B of the new-learner route: understand and use single words and formulaic phrases about very familiar things, with pictures and gestures. A1 is the target by the end of Band C for a beginner who did the whole course (an assumption, not a measured result).
- ТРКИ is an adult and school exam system (DP21); a children's everyday-Russian test exists from age 4 (SPBU). Neither gives a syllabus for 3–8, so I use them only as a reference for level names in the parent area.
- For classroom learners, **amount and quality of input** matters more than starting age, and older starters progress faster at first (MUN). For an app with 8–15-minute sessions, that means: lots of listening to meaningful, picturable language; repetition across sessions (the app's spaced revisits); and modest goals for the beginner route.
- Meaning before form: total physical response for commands (ASH), picture stories read aloud with talk (MOL), songs and rhymes as practice wisdom.

### 3.5 Writing on a phone: print first, free writing after tracing
- Children who **write letters by hand** learn to recognise them better than those who type (LON, though a replication was neutral). Five-year-olds' brains responded like readers' only after **free printing**, not after tracing or typing (JAM). So tracing is a step, not the goal: the app should move from tracing with a guide, to a start dot only, to **writing from memory**.
- Russian schools teach **cursive** (прописи) from grade 1 alongside printed letters for reading (FRP-NOO, second-hand). I found **no evidence** that cursive-first helps children of 5–8 learning Russian as a second or heritage language, and cursive Cyrillic differs a lot from print (т, д, и, п, г, з). Our learners read print, the app's tracer works with strokes in a box, and a finger on glass is poor for joined writing. **Decision: print only (printed capital and small letters), with cursive out of scope for now** (owner question 3).
- Spelling is separated from handwriting: building words from **letter tiles** (the Russian "разрезная азбука", cut-out alphabet) lets a 5-year-old spell before their hand can write.

### 3.6 Typical errors of Hebrew-speaking children (contrastive, evidence thin)
No study of Hebrew-speaking children **learning** Russian was found; the studies are of Russian-Hebrew bilinguals (SKW, MEIR, ROD). The list below is therefore built from the two sound systems (YAN, LAU), from what Russian teachers report for all beginners, and from the bilingual studies; each is a **hypothesis to watch**, tagged so the app can count it.

| Gender agreement (моя́ мяч) | Heritage and new learners (ROD). | M-RUS-GENDER |
| Nominative for accusative (я ви́жу ко́шка) | Heritage Russian in Israel (MEIR). | M-RUS-ACC |
| Number + noun (пять кот) | Russian number agreement. | M-RUS-NUMNOUN |

Code-mixing a Hebrew word into a Russian sentence is **normal bilingual behaviour, not an error**; the app never marks it.

### 3.7 Placement
- The Russian proficiency test for multilingual children (GAG10) shows what a placement should sample: vocabulary (nouns and verbs), morphology (gender, case, number), comprehension of constructions, plus a parent questionnaire about the child's languages. It takes about 60 minutes with a trained tester; ours must take **3–5 minutes per strand**, as a game, adaptive (`docs/curriculum.md` §10.1).
- Our placement uses: a short parent questionnaire (enums only: who speaks Russian at home; does the child understand, speak, know letters, read) to choose where the staircase starts, then five short staircases: understanding, sounds, letters, reading, spelling. No results are shown to the child; the parent sees "we start here" per strand.

## 4. Where the sources disagree or the evidence is thin
1. **Letter order.** Goretsky (vowels first, then frequency), Zhukova (А, У, О, М, С… stretchable first) and Elkonin (vowel letters in pairs, sound analysis long before letters) differ. None has been compared in a trial that I found. I chose a hybrid (syllabus › Letter order) and say why per group.
2. **Hard/soft for Hebrew speakers.** No study with Hebrew-speaking learners. I give soft consonants early (through и in group 3) and keep a listening skill for the contrast in Band A, because the contrast carries meaning and is new to Hebrew ears. This is a reasoned choice, not an evidenced one.
3. **Cursive.** Russian schools teach it from grade 1; no evidence for or against for our learners; I chose print only for now.
4. **Tracing.** One small fMRI study says tracing alone does not build letter knowledge the way free writing does (JAM); behavioural results are mixed (LON and its replication). I use tracing only as a scaffold.
5. **TPR, songs and rhymes.** Widely used, old or thin evidence for 3–4-year-olds in apps. Kept as practice, measured by the app's own mastery data.
6. **Heritage programmes for ages 3–8.** No researched curriculum found; the route is built from principles.
7. **How much Hebrew.** No study tells how much L1 support is best in an app for 3–8. I chose: instructions in Hebrew (owner's decision), the target language only in Russian, meaning carried by pictures not translation, and less Hebrew for heritage learners (owner question 2).
8. **Speech.** Children's speech recognition is unreliable at 4–6 (`docs/curriculum.md` principle 9), and there is no Russian recogniser in the app. Speaking is modelled and practised, never scored, until a Russian recogniser is approved and tested.

## 5. Decisions taken in the syllabus
1. Sound-analytic method (Elkonin–Zhurova) for literacy: sounds and the sound scheme before letters; syllable merge as the reading unit; the vowel strip for hard/soft.
2. Letters in 10 groups of 3–4, ordered by sound ease, frequency and separation of look-alikes and sound-alikes (syllabus › Letter order).
3. Three routes by placement: new learner, heritage, reader; five entry points E0–E4.
4. Print writing only: trace → start dot → write from memory; spelling with letter tiles from Band B.
5. Grammar only as chunks with pictures; the heritage route adds playful form-focus on gender, accusative and number + noun.
6. ё is written everywhere it belongs, in every text a child sees (LOP).
7. Stress: always right in the voice; stress marks shown to the child only in the stress skills and, for the new-learner route, on new words in Band C reading (owner question 6).
